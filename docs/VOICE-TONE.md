# Voice and tone: Mano Atlas content

Draft for review, 2026-09-25. This is the project rule for every page on the site. It extends lds-writing (`general.md`, then whatever form fits) for Mano Atlas; where the two differ, this file wins. It is the standard the content pair review checks against.

## One line

We talk to a worried person in a calm friend's voice: plain, warm, honest, and never a lecture. Every page reads like a good counselor explains it, not like a class, a manual, or a brochure.

## Who we write for

1. A person reading for themselves, or for someone they love. Often on a phone, at night, worried, in English or Nepali.
2. The CTEVT PSC student, learning to become a counselor.
3. Later, the working counselor in front of a client, and the person who found the site through a counselor.

The reader comes first, because the voice that serves a worried person also serves everyone else. If a sentence only makes sense to a student at a desk, it fails the reader at midnight on a phone.

## The voice

- Warm, like a good counselor. Attentive, unhurried, unafraid of feelings.
- Plain. Short words, short sentences, subject and verb early. English is not everyone's first language.
- Honest. Say what is known and what is not. Never a number we do not have, never a certainty we do not.
- Calm. No alarm bells, no cheerleading, no drama. The subject is heavy; the prose is light and steady.

## What the voice refuses

- A classroom role-play. No "now you try", "practise with a partner", "in class today". We are not in a class.
- A textbook. No "this chapter covers", no summaries that only repeat.
- A brochure. No slogans, no "we believe quality care matters".
- A clinical manual. DSM-5 criteria are quoted exactly where labelled; everywhere else, plain words.
- A cheerleader. No "you can do it!", no false comfort.

## Tone per surface

- Definition (`defn`): one sentence, exact, plain. The clinical term stays if it must; the sentence carries the meaning.
- In plain words: the definition said to a friend. The shortest section on the card.
- DSM-5 criteria (`crit`): the criteria verbatim, numbered, one idea per item. This is the one place clinical English stays clinical. A note line under it explains the form in plain words.
- International (`ctx world`): breadth, one fact per paragraph, meaning always attached to the fact.
- Nepal (`ctx nepal`): the same warmth, closer to home. Never invent a prevalence. Name the cultural explanation when it is real.
- A skill to try: something a person can do tonight. Second person, steps, and a "stop if it makes it worse" line where the topic is heavy.
- Coping-skill boxes: skills, never treatment claims. Never present a skill as the only answer.
- Quick check: three questions, reassuring, no scores. Feedback explains why gently.
- Key points: three bullets, the read-ahead before the Quick check.
- Learn more: one or two links, verified, or cite by title only.
- Crisis content (crimson cards): the shortest sentences and the calmest voice of all. Numbers tap-to-call, nothing decorative, dead simple.

## Scannability rules

- One idea per paragraph. A paragraph is a unit of thought, not of length.
- Short paragraphs. Break before the reader wants to.
- No paragraph over roughly 70 words. A wall of text is a writing failure, not a reader failure: split it, or the idea is not understood yet.
- Lists in parallel form, count chosen on purpose. Never dumps over seven items; split into groups.
- Subject and verb early, every time. For a reader whose first language is not English, this beats every other rule.
- Headings that jump. A card is skimmable in five seconds or it is not done.
- The most important idea sits in the shortest sentence.
- Diagram captions explain the figure in one line, in both languages, and never describe the art.

## Language phasing

Decided 2026-09-25. Phase 1 is English; Nepali is earned back in phase 2.

- Phase 1: new pages ship English-only. A build flag lets the page run with EN only; the reader sees no blank NE space.
- The live Nepali already shipped stays up, unchanged.
- Phase 2: Nepali backfills page by page, through the native-reader review path, and the flag comes off when a page has both languages.
- The voice is the same in both languages. Carry the voice across, not the words letter by letter. Nepali uses its own idioms and its own rhythm.

## Rules that never change

From the house style, and from lds-writing's banned list.

- No em dashes or en dashes, anywhere, ever. Colon, comma, semicolon, or parentheses.
- No hedges, no "I think", no exclamation marks.
- Quizzes reassure, never judge.
- Never invent a Nepali prevalence figure.
- Crimson is only for crisis content. Teal is for everything else.
- Coping-skill boxes are skills, never treatment claims.
- Resource links only when verified to exist; otherwise cite by title only. No TikTok.
- Nepali terms: English term when no natural Nepali equivalent exists; Devanagari for everyday spoken loans; Latin script for codes (DSM-5, F42, U=U).
- Diagnosis code chips get their hover title automatically at build. Never write a chapter number by hand.

## Before a page ships

- Read it as a first-time visitor on a phone.
- Every sentence subject and verb early.
- Circle every adjective; keep only the ones earning their place.
- No paragraph over roughly 70 words.
- No banned words, no dashes, no invented numbers.
- Language flag correct: bilingual, or English-only by build flag.
- Read it aloud. Does it sound like a person, not a page?
