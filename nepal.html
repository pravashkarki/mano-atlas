The Nepal panorama

The cross-cutting Nepali material every chapter leaned on, gathered in one place: the ethnopsychology, the idioms of distress, and the history of mental health care.

Idioms of distress: hear these, think of these

Man dukhne / man bhari
heart-mind aching / heavy → depression screen
Man aattine
heart-mind panicking → anxiety, panic
Jham-jham
tingling-numbness in limbs → anxiety, hyperventilation, somatic presentation
Jiu galne / kamjori
body wilting / weakness → depression, GAD, anaemia work-up
Saato jaane
soul flees after fright → acute stress, trauma; healer-treated
Chhopne / lagne
possession-like state / spirit affliction → conversion, dissociation, or culturally normal trance
Nindra nalagne
no sleep → the universal doorway complaint

Mental health care in Nepal: the timeline (from MH-3)

  • Pre-1960s: distress read through karma and spirits; dhami-jhankri and elders as first help
  • 1961–62: first psychiatric OPD, Bir Hospital (Dr. Bishnu Prasad Sharma)
  • 1984–85: Mental Hospital, Lagankhel, Patan
  • 1996: first National Mental Health Policy (primary-care integration)
  • 2020: National Mental Health Strategy & Action Plan: community psychosocial workers, task-sharing; the policy ground your own profession stands on

Culture-bound syndromes (DSM-5: "cultural concepts of distress") & healing systems

CTEVT PSC curriculum

CTEVT PSC curriculum: on the official Psychosocial Counselor syllabus, can appear in the exam. Beyond curriculum: added for completeness, not examinable. A chip like 296.2x · F32 is the diagnosis code: the DSM-5's older ICD-9-CM number, then the ICD-10 "F-code" used in hospital records. Non-students can ignore all of these.

The curriculum names three: dhat, possession, soul loss. A culture-bound syndrome is a pattern of distress recognised and named by a culture, coherent inside its own explanatory world.

Healing systems your clients use, usually before you:

  • Dhami-jhankri: shamanic diagnosis and ritual
  • Jharphuk: sweeping or blowing away affliction
  • Vaidya and Ayurveda
  • Lamas and monasteries, temple vows and pujas

The evidence-based stance is collaboration: healers refer what they cannot heal when they are respected, and families accept counselling more easily when it does not demand they abandon their world.

The counselor's line: work alongside any practice that is not harmful; act on harmful ones (beating "spirits" out, chaining, denial of urgent medical care): through relationship where possible, through protection systems where necessary.

Composite scenario. A client says, "man dukhne chha" (the heart-mind aches).

  • The counselor opens with this local term rather than labeling it depression
  • Reasoning: using the client's own words builds trust and honors the cultural framework
  • Outcome: the client shares more about sleep loss and appetite, and the counselor can screen accurately without imposing a Western category first

The standing rule: never invent Nepali prevalence figures. The National Mental Health Survey measured some conditions and not others, so quote the survey for what it carries (anxiety and OCD, each 0.2% current) and say plainly that no national figure exists for eating disorders and sleep disorders. Where Nepali evidence does exist (post-earthquake distress studies, the maternal-mortality suicide finding, the National Mental Health Survey 2020), cite it by name and year.

Key points
  1. Nepali distress speaks through the 'man' (heart-mind), the body, and spirit idioms: 'man dukhcha', 'sato gayo', 'chhopne'.
  2. Traditional healers are the first contact for most families; collaborate with them, do not compete.
  3. Formal care is young (Bir OPD 1961, Lagankhel 1985, policy 1996, strategy 2020) and thin outside cities; task-sharing fills the gap.
