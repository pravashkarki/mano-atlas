Mental health & mental illness: the basics

Before any disorder can be named, three questions have to be answered: what is mental health, where is the line between normal and abnormal, and what causes the line to be crossed. This is the CTEVT curriculum's opening block (M2 · Mental Health · Tasks 1–2).

Definitions, and the normal–abnormal line

Mental health (WHO/your class notes): a state of psychological, emotional, and social well-being in which a person realises their own potential, copes with the normal stresses of life, works productively, and contributes to their community. Mental illness: a clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotional regulation, or behaviour that causes distress or impairment in functioning. Health is not merely the absence of illness: a person can lack a diagnosis and still be languishing.

Where is the "abnormal" line? No single test decides it. Clinicians triangulate with the 4 D's:

  • Deviance: far outside cultural norms
  • Distress: it hurts
  • Dysfunction: it breaks daily functioning (work, school, relationships, self-care)
  • Danger: risk to self or others

Culture sits inside every D: hearing a dead relative's voice during mourning is deviant in Berlin and expected in Bhaktapur. Frequency, intensity, and duration turn an experience into a symptom.

MythFact
Mental health problems come from personal weakness or weak willpowerThey result from interacting biological, psychological, and environmental factors
Counselling is only for "severe" or "mad" casesCounselling helps with life transitions, stress, grief, trauma, and everyday emotional regulation
People with mental health conditions cannot workWith support, most work productively and thrive
Children do not get mental health problemsDistress can emerge at any age; early intervention improves lifelong outcomes
Mental illness is a spirit's curse or the fruit of karmaIt is a health condition; respectful bridge-building with traditional explanations helps families seek care sooner

What causes mental illness: two models to memorise

1 · The biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977). Every case has three strands braided together:

  • Biological (genes, brain chemistry, physical illness, substances)
  • Psychological (coping style, thought patterns, self-concept, learning history)
  • Social (poverty, family conflict, displacement, discrimination, support networks)

The curriculum's own list (biological, psychosocial, environmental) is this model in different words.

2 · The diathesis-stress model. A disorder appears when an underlying vulnerability (diathesis: genetic loading, early adversity, temperament) meets enough stress (loss, migration, exam pressure, disaster). High vulnerability needs little stress; low vulnerability needs a lot. This is why one sibling breaks after an earthquake and another does not, and why "why now?" is one of assessment's most powerful questions.

IndividualGenetic predisposition, chronic illness, low emotional regulationProblem-solving capacity, resilience, physical health

StructuralPoverty, discrimination, displacement, no access to careEconomic stability, safe environment, social inclusion
Test yourself: three questions

1) Name the 4 D's and give a Nepali example of each. 2) A woman hears her late husband's voice during the 13-day mourning ritual, which D's are absent, and why does that matter? 3) Use diathesis-stress to explain why two survivors of the same landslide can have opposite outcomes.

Keeping a mind well: one thinking skill and ten habits

Cognitive restructuring is the skill of catching a thought, checking it, and replacing it with a fairer one. The two distortions the class notes name (two of the five listed on Approaches & forms): catastrophising ("I failed one paper, my life is finished") and black-and-white thinking ("if I am not the best I am nothing"). The check is three questions: what is the evidence, what would I tell a friend, what is the most likely outcome. It pairs with the body skills on Tools & techniques (breath, muscle relaxation, 5-4-3-2-1 grounding) and with behavioural activation on Depressive disorders.

HabitWhy it holds a mind up
Sleep on a schedule, 7 to 9 hoursSleep is when the brain files emotion and resets stress hormones (Sleep-wake disorders)
Move every dayWalking, farm work, yoga: movement lifts mood through the body's own chemistry
A daily calming practiceSlow breathing or muscle relaxation keeps the alarm system from idling high
Keep people closeBelonging is the strongest single buffer against distress
BoundariesBeing able to say no protects time, energy and self-respect
Eat and drink regularlyStable blood sugar and water; go easy on caffeine and alcohol
Face problems, don't dodge themSelf-compassion and problem-solving beat avoidance, which grows anxiety
Limit screens and newsEspecially before bed: less overload, less rumination
Do something that mattersHobbies, creativity, helping: purpose and a sense of mastery
Ask for help earlyDistress caught early is easier to turn; that is what this course exists for
Key points
  1. Mental health is more than the absence of illness: it is coping, working, relating and contributing.
  2. The normal-abnormal line is drawn by distress, dysfunction, duration and danger, never by being different.
  3. Illness comes from biology, mind and social world together (biopsychosocial), often as a vulnerability meeting stress.
