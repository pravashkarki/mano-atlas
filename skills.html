Communication skills: SOLER and the seventeen

99 of your 780 hours go here, more than any disorder gets, because skills, not knowledge, are what the client experiences. Non-verbal skills open the door; verbal skills do the work.

The non-verbal foundation

CTEVT PSC curriculum

CTEVT PSC curriculum: on the official Psychosocial Counselor syllabus, can appear in the exam. Beyond curriculum: added for completeness, not examinable. A chip like 296.2x · F32 is the diagnosis code: the DSM-5's older ICD-9-CM number, then the ICD-10 "F-code" used in hospital records. Non-students can ignore all of these.

Before a single skill, the body is already speaking. Non-verbal presence does three jobs: it makes the room feel safe, it shows congruence, and it co-regulates.

It makes the room safe because a calm, receptive stance tells a nervous client that nothing here will be judged. It shows congruence: when your words and your posture agree, the client trusts both; when they disagree, the client believes the posture. It co-regulates: a grounded posture, steady breathing and a gentle voice slowly settle an activated client, the way a calm adult settles a frightened child.

SOLER

  • Sit squarely facing the client
  • Open posture (no crossed arms)
  • Lean slightly toward them
  • Eye contact, natural and culturally tuned
  • Relax

Around it sit three more channels.

  • Affirming: nodding and humming "hmm"
  • Silence: the hardest skill, waiting while the client finds words
  • Observation: posture, tears held back, hands, and what is not said

Nepali tuning matters here. Sustained direct eye contact from a young counselor to an elder, or across gender, can read as disrespect; soften it. A client may talk most freely while looking away.

Beyond SOLER, four channels carry most of what a client reads in you. None of them needs a number attached; they need attention.

A worked example: A counselor named Sunita meets a client who speaks softly and avoids eye contact. Sunita sits at an angle, keeps her hands visible, and lets silence stand for ten seconds. The client gradually says, "I have never told anyone this." The non-verbal choices made space; the words followed.

ChannelDoWatch out
Body and faceSmall, timed nods that say "go on"; a face that stays warm and matches the client's tone; let your posture follow theirs a little rather than sit like a statueThe flicker of shock, disgust or judgement on your face when trauma or a stigmatised act is disclosed; the client sees it before you feel it
VoiceWarm, unhurried; slow down and soften in the intense moments; let a pause stand, the client is working in itFilling every silence; speeding up when you are anxious; a bright cheerful tone over heavy material
SpaceAbout an arm's length or more, tuned to the person and the culture; sit at an angle or side by side, nothing between you: no desk, no file on your lapA desk between you turns the session into an interview; crowding a trauma survivor, or a woman meeting a male counselor, closes the door
TouchOnly if culturally safe and asked for. Warmth travels through voice, face and posture; those are enoughA hand on the shoulder can revive a trauma, blur the boundary, or offend across gender or caste; when in doubt, offer water instead
Practice drill: watch yourself

Record a ten-minute role-play, or ask a classmate to watch, and tick honestly:

  • Face and eyes
    • Eye contact soft and steady, not a stare, not darting away
    • Face warm and matching the client's tone
    • No flicker of shock or judgement at the hard moments
    • Nods small and timed
  • Posture and space
    • Body turned toward the client, or at a comfortable angle
    • Arms and legs uncrossed
    • A slight lean when the material gets heavy
    • Nothing between you
  • Voice
    • Calm and warm
    • Slower and quieter when the client is upset
    • Pauses allowed to stand
  • Presence and habits
    • No pen-clicking, foot-tapping, clock-checking
    • Phone silent and out of sight
    • Comfort offered through voice and posture, not touch

The verbal seventeen: with one example each

Nine of these seventeen are worked in full, with steps, phrases and the mistakes to avoid: the five reflecting skills on Listening back: reflecting skills, the four change skills on Moving forward: change skills. The other eight live only in this table.

SkillWhat it isSounds like
QuestioningOpen questions invite; closed questions confirm"What was that time like for you?" vs "Did you sleep?"
ParaphrasingTheir content, your fresh words"So since the loan came due, the nights are the worst."
Reflection of feelingNaming the emotion under the words"You sound frightened, and a little angry too."
Reflection of meaningNaming what it means to them"Failing the exam feels like failing your whole family."
Repetition of key wordsEcho the loaded word to open itClient: "…then everything finished." You: "Finished?"
SummarisingGathering threads at turns and closings"Let me pull together the three things you've named…"
ChallengeGently naming a contradiction"You say it doesn't matter, yet you cry when you say it."
FeedbackSharing what you observe, without verdicts"Today you looked at the floor when your father came up."
Information givingAccurate, needed, digestible facts"Panic symptoms are frightening but not dangerous: here is why."
Self-disclosureA brief, purposeful sliver of your own experience: for the client's sake, rarely"I too left my village for work; the first months were hard."
PsychoeducationTeaching the mechanism behind the symptomThe fight-or-flight body map of Fear: the foundation, drawn on paper
BrainstormingGenerating options before judging any"List every possibility first, even the silly ones."
Suggest and fadeOffer an option, hand ownership back"Some people try X: what would fit you?"
EmpathyFeeling-with communicated accuratelyNot "I understand" but proving it in your reflection
Consoling & appropriate touchWater, tissue, presence; touch only if culturally safeSilence + offered water often consoles more than words
Interpreting non-verbalsReading and gently naming body signals"Your fists closed when we mentioned the school."
Active listeningThe umbrella: all of the above, together, honestlyThe client's test: "Did this person actually hear me?"
Practice drill

Client says: "Since my husband went to Qatar I don't sleep, my chest burns, and my mother-in-law says I am pretending." Write one open question, one paraphrase, one reflection of feeling, one reflection of meaning, and one gentle challenge: then check each against the table.

Key points
  1. Skills, not knowledge, are what the client experiences; 99 of the 780 course hours are spent here.
  2. Non-verbal attending (posture, eye contact, silence) opens the door; verbal skills (reflecting, paraphrasing, open questions, summarising) do the work.
  3. Reflect feeling before content; the client should hear their own words come back warmer and clearer.
